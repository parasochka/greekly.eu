Greek A2 level: what it means and how to reach it

A2 is the “everyday survival plus” level of the European framework. Here is what it covers in Greek, the grammar and topics behind it, and a practical way to get there.

Updated September 30, 2026 · WCBO LLC

Short answer

A2 is the level where you can manage everyday routine in Greek: introduce yourself, shop, make appointments, ask for directions, talk simply about your job, family and plans, and read or write short messages. Reaching it takes a solid base of grammar (genders, three cases, present, past and future), a working vocabulary for daily life, and regular practice in all four skills.

What A2 means

The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) describes six levels, from A1 to C2. A2 is the second one, sometimes called “waystage”. In the CEFR's own terms, an A2 learner can:

  • understand sentences and frequently used expressions about areas of immediate relevance, such as personal and family information, shopping, the local area and work;
  • communicate in simple and routine tasks that need a direct exchange of information on familiar matters;
  • describe in simple terms their background, immediate environment and immediate needs.

In Greece and Cyprus, A2 is also a level people certify, for example for residence paperwork. If that is your goal, read Greek A2 exam next.

Greek grammar for A2

  • Nouns and articles: the three genders, singular and plural, definite and indefinite articles (ο, η, το; ένας, μία, ένα).
  • Cases: nominative, accusative and the most common uses of the genitive (το σπίτι της Μαρίας).
  • Adjectives: agreement with the noun, simple comparatives (πιο μεγάλος).
  • Verbs: the two main present-tense patterns (γράφω, μιλάω), είμαι and έχω, the simple past (aorist), the future with θα, να + subjunctive (θέλω να πάω), and basic commands.
  • Verb aspect: the difference between continuous and one-off actions (θα γράφω / θα γράψω).
  • Pronouns: personal pronouns, weak object pronouns (με, σε, τον, την, το), possessives (μου, σου, του).
  • Small words that do a lot: prepositions σε, από, με, για, question words, negation with δεν and μην.
  • Numbers, dates, time and prices.

A practical plan to get there

  1. Get the base right. Alphabet, stress, articles, present tense. Our guide How to learn Greek shows the order.
  2. Practise every day, briefly. 15 to 20 minutes of quizzes, words and sentence-building beats occasional long sessions.
  3. Write something short every day. Two or three sentences about your day. Writing is where grammar becomes automatic.
  4. Work through everyday dialogues for the topics above: the pharmacy, the bank, the landlord, the bus.
  5. Listen daily to slow, clear Greek: learner podcasts, short videos, the news for learners if you can find it.
  6. Speak weekly with a tutor or a language partner, and use Greek in real life whenever you can.

Frequently asked questions

What does A2 mean in Greek?

A2 is the second of six levels in the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR). At A2 you can understand frequently used expressions about everyday life, handle simple routine exchanges, and describe your background, surroundings and immediate needs in simple terms.

Is A2 Greek enough to live in Greece or Cyprus?

It is enough for many routine situations: shops, appointments, simple messages, small talk. It is not enough to follow fast conversation or complex paperwork comfortably. Many learners treat A2 as a milestone on the way to a confident everyday level.

What grammar do you need for Greek A2?

Articles and the three genders, nominative, accusative and genitive, the present, simple past (aorist) and future, να with the subjunctive, basic commands, weak object pronouns, possessives, comparatives, prepositions, numbers, dates and time.

How long does it take to reach A2 in Greek?

It depends on your starting point and how much you practise. With regular daily practice and some speaking, many adult beginners reach A2 in months rather than years. The alphabet and cases make Greek slower at the start than languages written in Latin script.
