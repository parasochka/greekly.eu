How to learn Greek: a step-by-step plan from zero to real conversations

A practical route for adult beginners who want to speak Modern Greek in everyday life: what to learn first, what to leave for later, and how to practise a little every day.

Updated September 30, 2026 · WCBO LLC

Short answer
  1. Learn the Greek alphabet and the stress mark first (a few days).
  2. Memorise 50 to 100 survival phrases and the most common words.
  3. Learn grammar in this order: articles and gender, present tense, the accusative, the past tense, the future with θα.
  4. Practise 15 to 20 minutes every day: short quizzes, writing, and dialogues about your own life.
  5. Add listening and speaking as early as you can: Greek radio, podcasts, a tutor, and real conversations.

Which Greek to learn

When people say “Greek” today they mean Modern Greek (Νέα Ελληνικά), the official language of Greece and, together with Turkish, of the Republic of Cyprus. It is spoken by about 13 million people, including large communities in Australia, the United States, the UK and Germany.

Modern Greek is not the same as Ancient Greek or Koine (Biblical) Greek. They share the alphabet and a lot of vocabulary, but pronunciation and grammar differ enough that a course in one does not teach you to speak the other. If your goal is to live, work or travel in Greece or Cyprus, you want Modern Greek. This guide is about Modern Greek only.

In Cyprus people speak Cypriot Greek day to day, while schools, news and official documents use Standard Modern Greek. Start with the standard language: every Cypriot understands it. More on that in Learn Greek in Cyprus.

Step 1. The alphabet and pronunciation

The Greek alphabet has 24 letters. About a third look and sound familiar (Α, Ε, Κ, Μ, Ο, Τ), some look familiar but sound different (Η is “i”, Ρ is “r”, Ν is “n”, Χ is a throaty “kh”), and the rest are new (Γ, Δ, Θ, Λ, Ξ, Π, Σ, Φ, Ψ, Ω).

Three things make reading easier than it looks:

  • Spelling is regular. Once you know the rules you can read any word aloud correctly, even if you do not know what it means. The opposite is harder: the sound “i” can be written ι, η, υ, ει, οι, so spelling words is a skill you build over time.
  • Every word of two or more syllables shows its stress with an accent mark (τόνος): καλημέρα, ευχαριστώ. Stress changes meaning, so read it every time.
  • A few letter pairs make single sounds: ου = “oo”, αι = “e”, μπ = “b”, ντ = “d”, γκ = “g”.

Tip: learn the alphabet by reading real words, not by reciting letters. Shop signs, street names and menus are perfect practice once you can sound out a word.

Step 2. Survival phrases and high-frequency words

Before any grammar, learn a small set of phrases you will use every day: greetings, please and thank you, “I don't understand”, ordering a coffee, asking how much something costs. They give you instant wins and a feel for how Greek sounds. Our list of basic Greek phrases has 70 of them with pronunciation.

Then build vocabulary around your own life rather than a textbook family: your job, your neighbourhood, your food, your plans for the weekend. Words you actually need stick much faster than words you might need one day.

Step 3. Grammar in the right order

Greek grammar has a reputation, but it is systematic. The trick is to learn it in an order where each step is immediately useful.

  1. Articles and gender. Every noun is masculine, feminine or neuter, and the article shows which: ο καφές (the coffee), η θάλασσα (the sea), το νερό (the water). Learn every noun with its article.
  2. The present tense. Most verbs follow two main patterns: μένω (I live) and μιλάω (I speak). Add the irregular essentials: είμαι (I am), έχω (I have), πάω (I go), θέλω (I want).
  3. The accusative case. The form a noun takes as an object or after most prepositions: θέλω έναν καφέ, πάω στη θάλασσα. With the nominative, this covers most of what you say at the start.
  4. Εσύ and εσείς. Greek has an informal “you” (εσύ) and a polite or plural one (εσείς). Use εσείς with strangers, officials and older people.
  5. The past tense (aorist). Πήγα (I went), έφαγα (I ate), μίλησα (I spoke). The moment you can talk about yesterday, conversations open up.
  6. The future and να. Θα πάω (I will go), θέλω να πάω (I want to go). Greek has no infinitive, so να + verb does a lot of work.
  7. Verb aspect. Greek verbs have two stems: one for ongoing or repeated actions and one for single, completed ones. Θα γράφω (I will be writing, regularly) versus θα γράψω (I will write, once). This is the biggest new idea for English speakers, and it comes naturally with practice.
  8. The genitive and weak pronouns. Το σπίτι μου (my house), σε ξέρω (I know you), του το έδωσα (I gave it to him).

Step 4. Practise a little, every day

Consistency beats intensity. Fifteen to twenty minutes a day will get you further than a three-hour session on Sunday, because memory needs repetition spread over time. A good daily routine mixes four kinds of practice:

  • Recall: quizzes and flashcards that make you retrieve words and forms, with the ones you got wrong coming back more often.
  • Production: building sentences and writing short messages in Greek. Recognising the right answer is not the same as producing it.
  • Context: reading or listening to short dialogues about situations you actually meet: the bakery, the pharmacy, the landlord.
  • Feedback: an explanation for every mistake, so an error turns into a mini-lesson.

Step 5. Listening, speaking and real life

Apps and books build the base. Speaking and listening need real Greek:

  • Listen daily to something slightly above your level: Greek radio (ERT), podcasts for learners, Greek music with lyrics, series with Greek subtitles.
  • Speak early. A weekly session with a tutor or a language exchange partner will speed you up more than anything else once you know a few hundred words.
  • Use Greek where you live. Order in Greek, greet neighbours in Greek, read your utility bill in Greek. Greeks and Cypriots switch to English quickly; a friendly στα ελληνικά, παρακαλώ (in Greek, please) usually keeps the conversation going.

Is Greek hard to learn?

Honestly: harder than Spanish, easier than people fear. The US Foreign Service Institute places Greek among the languages that take English speakers about 44 weeks (roughly 1,100 class hours) of full-time study to reach professional working proficiency, alongside Russian and Hindi. That figure is for professional-level fluency; everyday conversation comes much sooner.

How long does it take?

There is no single number, because it depends on your starting point, how often you practise and what “learning Greek” means to you. A useful way to think about it:

  • Reading the alphabet: a few days.
  • Survival Greek (greetings, ordering, prices, directions): a few weeks of daily practice.
  • A confident everyday level (shops, doctor, landlord, small talk about work and family, simple messages): months of regular practice plus real conversations.
  • Professional fluency: years, in line with the FSI estimate above.

If you need a formal certificate, for example for residence paperwork, see our pages on the A2 level of Greek and the Greek A2 exam.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Staying on transliteration too long. Switch to Greek script in the first week.
  • Learning nouns without articles. Καφές is useless without knowing it is ο καφές.
  • Ignoring the stress mark. Πότε means “when”, ποτέ means “never”.
  • Only recognising, never producing. Write and build sentences, even short ones, every day.
  • Waiting to be “ready” to speak. You will never feel ready. Start with five sentences at the bakery.

Tools that help

  • An app for daily practice. Short sessions, explanations and repetition. What to look for in a Greek app.
  • A tutor or conversation partner for speaking, once a week if you can.
  • A grammar reference to look things up when a pattern confuses you.
  • Greek audio you enjoy: radio, podcasts, music, series.
  • Your own life as the syllabus: the more your practice is about your city, your work and your plans, the faster it sticks.

Frequently asked questions

Is Greek hard to learn for English speakers?

It takes longer than Spanish or French, but it is very learnable. The US Foreign Service Institute puts Greek in its group of languages that need about 44 weeks (around 1,100 class hours) of full-time study to reach professional working proficiency. The alphabet takes days, not months, and spelling is far more regular than English. The real work is grammar: three genders, noun cases and verb aspect.

How long does it take to learn Greek?

It depends on your goal and how often you practise. Reading the alphabet takes a few days. Handling everyday situations like shops, cafés, the doctor and small talk takes months of regular practice. Professional fluency is a matter of years. Short daily sessions beat long weekly ones.

Can I learn Greek on my own?

Yes, for reading, vocabulary, grammar and writing. An app, a textbook and Greek audio will take you a long way. For speaking you need other people: a tutor, a conversation partner, or simply using Greek in shops and cafés whenever you can.

Should I learn Modern Greek or Ancient Greek?

If you want to talk to people in Greece or Cyprus, learn Modern Greek. Ancient and Koine (Biblical) Greek are separate subjects with different grammar and pronunciation, studied for reading old texts.

Do I need to learn the Greek alphabet first?

Yes. It is 24 letters and most learners can read slowly within a few days. Learning with transliteration (Greek written in Latin letters) feels faster at first but slows you down later, because signs, menus and messages are all in Greek script.

What is the best app to learn Greek?

The best app is the one you open every day. Look for short sessions, explanations for every mistake, audio, and practice that produces Greek (writing and building sentences), not just tapping the right picture.
